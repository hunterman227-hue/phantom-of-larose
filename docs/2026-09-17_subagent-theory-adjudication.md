# Theory Adjudication — 2026 Mayflower Quarter Reverse (front of mast / foresail)
Date: 2026-09-17 | Adjudicator: subagent (theory review, photo evidence + written analyses only — no in-hand coin examined)

## The one fact all three theories agree on
The marks are **on the die** and **repeatable**. Pete Apple concluded this after working through planchet-only explanations and discarding them. David's core observation — the same feature appearing on multiple coins — is what establishes die origin. Randy Orre's reading also assumes die origin. **Repeatability is settled. Mechanism is not.**

## Theory 1 — David James: growing die chip → cracks → Phantom of LaRose
**Claim:** A die chip at the front of the mast grows into die cracks, forming a bird-like figure in stages: duck head → half phantom → full Phantom. Obverse Die-1 markers (Mosquito Bite, Splenter, Ant Bite) identify the die. Earlier he also entertained spalling, ghosting, die clash, feeder fingers, PIDT, over-tonnage, and strikethrough as possible mechanisms.

**What it predicts under magnification:**
- Sharp-edged, jagged raised lines (classic die-crack morphology) that lengthen/branch across die states.
- Discrete staging: early-state coins show only the "duck head" chip; later-state coins show longer cracks; latest show the full figure — all provably from the same working die.
- The obverse markers should co-occur with reverse stages on the same die pair.

**Evidence for:**
- Raised dots and a wavy raised ridge near the pilgrim's hand/arm are real, repeatable features across 10 obverse photos (cross-reference audit, 2026-09-16: high confidence the features exist).
- Pete Apple himself agrees the marks are on the die, real, and "a challenge when it comes to understanding die design."
- Pete's own Sept 1 CoinWeek article describes a "remarkably similar die event in front of the foresail" and calls it "an evolving event rather than a single isolated defect" — conceding change over die life, which is compatible with David's staging claim.
- Randy Orre independently sees crack-like features in the photos (see Theory 3).

**Evidence against / problems:**
- **No half-phantom or full-phantom stage is visible in any filed photo at usable magnification.** Only three reverse photos show anything in the zone (011, 013: possible early/duck-head, low–medium confidence; 014: possible middle-crack linear feature, low–medium confidence). The late stages exist only in photos not yet on file.
- **The First 500 finding:** 479 photos + 18 videos covering ~500 different coins from a multi-day audit shoot contain zero instances of the markers, phantom stages, or study-coin matches. If the progression were a common or long-lived die state, it should appear there. This suggests the feature — if real — is rare, confined to a narrow die state, or limited to a specific die pair the First 500 didn't include.
- The "it repeats perfectly, so it must be a crack" argument is a false dichotomy (see "The repeatability question" below) — flow lines repeat too.
- The auxiliary mechanisms David entertained (spalling, ghosting, clash, feeder fingers, PIDT, over-tonnage) were each rejected by Pete with specific technical reasoning; none is currently evidenced.
- No published variety listing (CONECA, Variety Vista, Wexler) as of 2026-09-16.

**What would falsify it:** Same-die overlays showing the "staged" photos come from different dies; or high-magnification showing smooth flow morphology with no broken metal; or a die-state sequence showing the feature stable rather than growing.

## Theory 2 — Pete Apple: die flow waves on the die
**Claim:** The marks are die flow waves — die flow taking the form of waves terminating in nodes — sitting on the die near the high-relief curve. Explicitly NOT spalling, ghosting, clash, feeder fingers, PIDT, or over-tonnage on Mayflower. He is not convinced die flow should be described as a "progression" the way a die crack would be. Coins technically interesting; no dollar value.

**What it predicts under magnification:**
- Smooth, wavy raised ridges with rounded edges and terminal nodes (lumps where waves end), following metal-flow direction — not sharp or jagged.
- The pattern is stable once formed: it does not lengthen or branch the way a crack does; it may strengthen slightly with die wear but does not "progress" through discrete stages.
- Location concentrated near the high-relief curve (foresail/mast area), where flow stress is highest.

**Evidence for:**
- **Morphology + location match:** the observed features are wavy ridges near a high-relief device — exactly where die flow concentrates. The obverse "Splenter" (wavy raised ridge along the arm) also reads as flow-like.
- **Sample size:** Pete has examined many examples across groups and his published work; his Sept 1 CoinWeek article documents the same foresail-zone event as an evolving die phenomenon.
- **Explanatory economy:** one mechanism (flow) accounts for both obverse ridges and reverse waves without needing chips, cracks, clashes, or transfer phenomena.
- His rule-outs are specific and reasoned (e.g., feeder fingers are "two totally separate events" from clash; Mayflower shows no spalling in any example he has seen).

**Evidence against / problems:**
- **Internal tension:** Pete's article calls the foresail event "an evolving event rather than a single isolated defect" and reconstructs die life from near-successive strikes — which is uncomfortably close to David's "progression" framing that Pete verbally rejects. If the event evolves across die life, the "not a progression" stance needs a clearer boundary.
- **Doesn't explain discrete staging** if David's duck-head → phantom photos (same die, ordered) hold up — flow waves don't typically form bird-like discrete figures. (Counter: the late-stage photos aren't on file, so this is currently untestable.)
- **Authority is not evidence:** Pete's experience and publication record make his read worth weight, but the mechanism still needs the microscopic test below. His "no $ value" is a market opinion, not a grading-service verdict — unlisted varieties can be real without being market-recognized.

**What would falsify it:** Sharp, jagged, branching raised lines with clear metal displacement (crack morphology); or a verified same-die sequence showing discrete growth stages; or a step-offset (bi-level) profile.

## Theory 3 — Randy Orre: (bi-level) die cracks — brittle fracture
**Claim (2026-09-17 message):** Many of the examples are die cracks of a rarer kind — **bi-level die cracks**. Ordinary die cracks appear as thin raised lines following an erratic path. The rarer type Randy thinks he sees here shows **a step instead of a raised line**, reflecting a slight sinking-in of the die face on one side of the crack. He considers these brittle-fracture manifestations, notes complex modern designs stress dies earlier on hard-metal coins like quarters, saw possible feeder finger marks in one photo but advises focusing on the bi-level investigation, and recommends an AmScope trinocular microscope.

**What it predicts under magnification:**
- A visible **step/offset**: one side of the linear feature sits slightly lower than the other — like a tiny cliff — rather than a raised wire sitting on the surface.
- Erratic, branching path consistent with brittle fracture (a snap, not wear).
- Under rotating light, one side of the line casts a shadow while the other does not (the signature of a step vs. a raised ridge, which casts shadows on both sides).

**Evidence for:**
- It **bridges the other two theories**: a crack-type mechanism (compatible with David's crack intuition) that explains why the features don't look like textbook raised-line cracks (compatible with Pete's observation that they don't look like ordinary cracks).
- The "hard metal coins like quarters" + complex-design stress argument is metallurgically plausible for modern clad coinage.
- Comes from someone with genuine variety experience (repunched-mintmark page activity; articles in progress, possible Coin World print).

**Evidence against / problems:**
- **Explicitly tentative and remote:** based on phone-screen photos from California ("as best I can tell from the pictures"), with no in-hand examination. Randy himself frames it as "what I see in some of the photos."
- **Rare mechanism invoked on thin evidence:** bi-level cracks are uncommon; claiming the rare variant needs stronger proof than phone photos.
- The feeder-finger observation is hedged ("what looks like") and he himself deprioritizes it.

**What would falsify it:** Raised-line-only profile with no step; smooth flow morphology; or the step turning out to be a lighting artifact (single-angle photos are notorious for this — the filed reverse photos are nearly all single-lighting-angle).

## Decisive tests — what would separate the three
These are ordered by diagnostic power. All require proper magnification (20–50x minimum, ideally the AmScope-type trinocular Randy recommends) and multi-angle lighting.

1. **Step vs. raised line vs. wavy ridge (the single most decisive test).** Rotate the coin under the light. A classic die crack = raised sharp line, shadows on both sides. A bi-level crack = step, shadow on one side only. Die flow = smooth wavy ridge with soft luster, minimal shadowing. This one observation eliminates at least one theory.
2. **Edge character.** Jagged, sharp, angular edges → crack/brittle fracture. Smooth, rounded, flowing edges → die flow.
3. **Broken vs. flowed metal.** Cracks displace metal; flow redistributes it. Look for luster disruption: cracks break luster lines; flow lines follow them.
4. **Same-die verification FIRST.** Overlay the "duck head," "half phantom," and "full phantom" photos (when the late-stage photos arrive). If they are not the same working die, the progression claim collapses regardless of mechanism. This must precede any staging argument.
5. **Die-state sequencing.** With same-die coins in plausible strike order: does the feature lengthen/branch discretely (crack progression, David/Randy) or stay essentially stable (flow, Pete)?
6. **Feeder-finger check.** Feeder fingers leave parallel, directional marks where the feeding mechanism contacts the planchet/die. An erratic, branching path argues against feeder fingers; straight parallel lines argue for them. Randy's own advice: don't spend effort here until the crack question is settled.
7. **Node check for flow.** Terminal nodes (small lumps where waves end) are Pete's predicted signature — look for them specifically.

## The repeatability question — why Pete's die flow waves would repeat "perfectly" coin to coin
Plain-language die mechanics, because this is the exact point of confusion:

- A coin die is a **stamp**. Every coin struck by that die receives **everything** on the die's face — design, cracks, flow lines, polish marks, all of it.
- Die flow lines are **wear carved into the die itself**. As thousands of planchets are struck, metal flow erodes microscopic waves into the die face near high-relief areas. Once those waves exist **on the die**, every subsequent coin shows them, identically, until the die is polished or retired.
- So "it repeats perfectly coin to coin" proves **the feature is on the die**. It does **not** tell you whether the feature is a crack or flow lines. Both live on the die. Both repeat. David's inference — repeatability ⇒ crack — is the step that doesn't follow, and it is the step Pete has been pushing back on.
- What repeatability rules out: planchet flaws, strike-throughs, and other one-coin accidents. What it cannot rule out: any die-level mechanism.

## Weighed assessment (no fluff)
- **Settled:** the features are real, on the die, and repeatable. All three agree. The obverse raised dots/ridge are high-confidence real; the reverse mast-zone features are low–medium confidence in the current photos.
- **Unresolved:** the mechanism. The filed photo evidence is insufficient to decide — single lighting angles, no late-stage phantom photos on file, no microscopic step-vs-ridge test performed.
- **Current lean, stated honestly:** Pete's die-flow read best fits the *wavy morphology and location* and carries the most examined examples, but it strains against his own published "evolving event" language and cannot yet account for discrete staging. Randy's bi-level crack is the most elegant bridge hypothesis but rests on the thinnest evidence (phone photos, explicitly qualified). David's chip→crack progression has a real die-origin observation at its core, but its signature claim — the staged Phantom growth — is currently unverifiable (stages not on file) and took a hit from the First 500 (zero instances in ~500 coins).
- **No winner can be honestly declared.** The next three moves, in order: (1) same-die overlay verification of the staged photos when the late-stage images arrive; (2) the microscopic step-vs-raised-line-vs-wavy-ridge test; (3) Bill Fivaz's verdict via Dennis Tucker. Any one of these could move the needle decisively.
- **On value:** Pete's "no dollar value" is an informed market opinion, not a technical falsification. Unlisted ≠ unreal. But nothing here is listable or valuable until a recognized authority attributes it — which is exactly what the Fivaz lunch is for.
